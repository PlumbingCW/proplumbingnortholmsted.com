DIY Sump Pump Installation in North Olmsted, OH

A sump pump is a manageable project for a handy homeowner, as long as you follow the city rules and size it right.

What a sump pump does

A sump pump sits in a pit below the basement floor. When groundwater fills the pit, a float switch turns the pump on and pushes the water out through a discharge pipe.

In a wet spring or after a storm, the pump may run every few minutes. If it fails, the basement can flood within hours, which is why sizing and backup power matter.

Before you dig: rules and permits

Check whether your project needs a permit. North Olmsted requires a permit before plumbing work begins, and an owner doing their own work needs an approved drawing, according to the city. The Building Division can explain which projects apply.

Where the water goes matters as much as the pump. Section 911.07(j) of the city code bars downspouts and footing drains from connecting to the sanitary sewer, so discharge must go outside the home to a lawful location.

The North Olmsted angle

North Olmsted note

Section 1339.01 of the North Olmsted codified ordinances requires a cover on a sump pump pit. The Building Division at 5200 Dover Center Road issues plumbing permits, and the city says owners doing their own plumbing must submit an approved drawing. Call the division before you buy parts so your plan, pit cover and discharge route match what the city will inspect.

Tools and parts list

ItemWhy you need it
Submersible pumpSized to the water you expect, with a vertical float if the pit is narrow
Pit liner with coverHolds the pump and meets the cover requirement
Check valveStops water in the discharge pipe from flowing back
PVC pipe and fittingsDischarge line to the outside
Gravel and sealantBed for the pit and a sealed lid
Battery backupKeeps the pump running in a power outage

Buy a pump with a rating that matches your inflow, not the cheapest one on the shelf.

The basic steps

1

Cut and dig

Break the slab where the pit goes and dig to the depth of the liner.

2

Set the liner

Place the liner on gravel, level it and pour concrete around it.

3

Install the pump

Place the pump on the bottom, connect the discharge pipe and check valve.

4

Test it

Pour water into the pit, confirm the float works and the water leaves the house.

Breaking concrete is hard, dusty work. Rent a demolition hammer, wear eye and ear protection and watch for floor drains and pipes under the slab.

Discharge: where the water goes

  • Run the pipe outside, above frost, and away from the foundation.
  • Do not connect to the sanitary sewer, because the code bars it.
  • Keep discharge off the neighbor property and public sidewalk.
  • Use a check valve near the pump to prevent backflow.
  • Add a freeze guard in winter, since ice can block the line.

A discharge that freezes closed makes the pump run and fill the pit. If you do not have a good outdoor route, a plumber can help you plan one.

When to hire a professional

Hire help if the slab is thick, there is no pit, the water table is high, or you need a battery backup and alarm. A pro can also size the pump and set a second pump for heavy flows.

Maintenance after the install

Test the pump at least a few times a year by pouring water into the pit. Clean debris from the pit and check the float. Replace the pump on the maker timetable, because pumps wear out.

A battery backup needs its own testing and a new battery every few years.

Sizing the pump

Pump size depends on how much water enters the pit and how high the pump must lift it. A bigger motor is not always better, because a pump that is too large cycles on and off quickly and wears out. Most home pumps are one third to one half horsepower.

To estimate inflow, watch how fast the pit fills after heavy rain. A pump that clears the pit in a minute and then waits a few minutes is a good match. If the pit refills faster than the pump can empty it, you need more capacity or a second pump.

Backup power and alarms

Storms that fill sump pits often knock out power at the same time. A battery backup pump takes over when the main pump or the power fails, and a water level alarm warns you before the basement floods.

A water-powered backup uses municipal pressure instead of a battery, but it uses a lot of water and may not be permitted everywhere. Ask the Building Division or your plumber before you choose one.

Common DIY mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is skipping the check valve. Without it, water in the discharge pipe falls back into the pit each time the pump stops, and the pump cycles more than it should. The second is a pit with no gravel base, which lets silt clog the inlet.

A third mistake is a discharge that ends too close to the house, so water runs back to the foundation and into the pit again. Extend the pipe well away from the wall and slope the ground so water moves off.

The fourth is ignoring the cover and the permit. The city sets rules for the cover and for inspection, and unpermitted work can cause trouble when you sell the house or file an insurance claim.

Testing for the next storm

Once the pump is in, test it on a dry day and again before the wet season. Pour a bucket of water into the pit and confirm the pump starts, runs and shuts off, with water leaving the discharge outside.

Plan to test the backup the same way. Unplug the main pump briefly to prove the battery unit takes over, then plug it back in.
