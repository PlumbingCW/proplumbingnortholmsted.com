Basement bathrooms and below-grade drains

A bathroom added below the sewer level often needs a sewage ejector pump. The pump lifts waste to the main drain. It also needs a sealed pit and vent.

Check with the city before you start. Plumbing work needs a permit before it begins, according to the North Olmsted permit page, and a drawing may be needed. We prepare it. Related drainage needs are covered by sump pump installation and repair.

Gas line work: safety first

If you smell gas, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 before calling us. Do not use switches or phones inside.

Gas lines for ranges, dryers, fireplaces, grills and water heaters need correct sizing, approved materials and a pressure test. Gas work requires a permit and inspection. We do not promise any response time for gas leaks. The utility is the first call.

Permits and inspection in North Olmsted

The city Building Division issues plumbing permits. The application says the permit holder arranges inspections with 24 hours notice, and a general building permit may need to be filed first for larger projects.

Homeowners who do their own plumbing must submit a drawing approved by the inspector. Contractors must be registered with the city. We handle the paperwork for jobs we do.

Backflow and code items

The city plumbing application asks for a backflow prevention certification number, which shows how seriously backflow is treated. Hose bibs, irrigation and some fixtures need backflow protection.

We install and can arrange testing for devices that need it. Commercial properties in particular should keep test records, covered on our commercial plumbing page.

New construction and additions

For new builds, plumbing is done in stages. Rough-in goes in before walls close. Inspection follows. Fixtures and final connections come last.

We coordinate with your builder and the building department so inspections do not hold up the schedule.

Choosing fixtures that fit your plumbing

The best fixture is one the existing plumbing can support. Rough-in dimensions, drain size, venting and supply pressure all matter, and a mismatch costs more to fix than the fixture saves.

  • Toilets need the right rough-in distance from wall to flange, commonly 12 inches in many homes but not all.
  • Showers and tubs need the correct valve depth for the trim you choose.
  • Sinks need a drain that is vented and sized for the fixture.
  • High-flow fixtures such as large rain showers need adequate supply pipe.

Send us the model number before you buy. We can check the specification against what is in the wall and save you a return trip.

How a remodel plumbing job is sequenced

1

Demolition

Walls and floors are opened, and old fixtures are removed.

2

Rough-in

New drain, vent and supply lines are run and pressure tested.

3

Inspection

The city inspector checks the rough-in before walls close.

4

Finish

Fixtures are set, connected and tested after drywall and flooring.

Plan for plumbing to be needed twice, once before walls close and once at the end. A schedule that shows both visits helps the other trades work around it.

Water pressure and supply sizing

Adding fixtures asks more of the supply line. A three-quarter inch main feeding a new bath is normal. A half-inch line serving a remodeled kitchen and bath together may not be.

We check static and flowing pressure before adding load. If pressure is low, the cause may be at the street or in the pipes, and water line repair explains what to check first.
