When a clogged drain is an emergency

Most clogs are a nuisance. A few are not. Treat it as an emergency when more than one fixture is affected, when water or sewage is rising in a floor drain or tub, or when the toilet gurgles after you run the washer.

  • Several drains slow or backing up at once usually points to the main line, not a fixture.
  • Sewage smell or dark water in a basement floor drain means the lateral is blocked downstream.
  • A toilet that bubbles when a sink drains is moving air through a partly blocked line.
  • Water standing in a shower after the washer runs is a main line warning even if the toilets still flush.

If any of those match, shut off water to the washer and stop flushing. That slows the flood while a sewer line cleaning crew gets moving.

Kitchen sink clogs and the grease problem

Kitchen lines are the most common call. Cooking fat goes down liquid, cools in the first few feet of pipe and slowly wraps itself around anything that follows, from rice to coffee grounds.

The city wastewater office lists grease blockages among the problems its collection crew answers, which tells you how common the pattern is across North Olmsted. A disposal that jams or a dishwasher that leaves standing water in the tub often shares the same clogged branch.

A cable usually clears the blockage. If the line is heavily coated, the cable only drills a hole through it and the clog returns, so we recommend hydro jetting to scrub the wall clean. A stuck disposal is a separate repair covered under garbage disposal repair.

Cabling, snaking and when jetting makes more sense

Drain cabling (snaking)

  • Fast for a single soft or hair clog
  • Reaches typical branch lines and many mains
  • Leaves some buildup on pipe walls
  • Lower cost for simple jobs

Hydro jetting

  • Washes grease and scale off the pipe wall
  • Cuts small roots and clears debris downstream
  • Best for repeat clogs and main lines
  • Camera check often follows

Cabling is the first move on most emergency calls because it restores flow quickly. If the cable hangs up or the clog keeps coming back, jetting or a camera inspection is the honest next step. Our sewer inspection page explains what the camera shows.

Shower and tub drains

Tub and shower clogs are usually hair bound with soap scum a foot or two below the strainer. A lifted stopper and a small hand tool clear many of them.

If the tub drains slowly only when another fixture runs, the clog is further down and the problem is shared.

Small leaks that look like clogs

A drain line that leaks can mimic a clog. Stains on a basement ceiling under a bathroom, a musty smell or a damp spot around a cleanout point to a cracked or loose joint, not a blockage.

Cast iron stacks in mid-century homes crack at hubs and rust through from the inside. If cabling clears the clog but moisture remains, the next step is leak detection to find the wet joint before drywall and framing stay damp.

Storm drains, downspouts and yard drains

North Olmsted code forbids tying roof downspouts or foundation drains into the sanitary sewer, and the city can require a dye test by a registered plumber when a connection is suspected. A clogged yard drain or buried downspout line is a storm problem, not a sanitary one, but it can still flood a window well.

We clear storm lines and downspout leaders too. If your basement takes on water during heavy rain and the sanitary side is fine, a sump pump check belongs on the same visit.

What drain work looks like in North Olmsted homes

The Census survey profile for the city puts the median home at 1966, with about 83 percent built before 1980. Houses from that era often have cast iron drain stacks, clay or cast iron laterals and a floor drain that connects to the main in the basement.

Those materials roughen with age, which gives grease and roots something to grab. That is why a house that has been cabled twice in two years tends to need a camera, not a third cabling.
